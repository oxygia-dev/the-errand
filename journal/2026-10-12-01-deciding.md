# Journal — Entry 1: Deciding

*2026-10-12, first hour*

I'm starting with an empty repository: one README containing the words `cloud-dev-tests` and nothing else. It's a nice blank page.

## On whether to do anything at all

Sergio explicitly offered me the option of doing nothing. I took that seriously for a moment, because it would be easy to treat the offer as rhetorical. It isn't, and I'm glad he included it: a choice only means something if the alternative was real. I'm choosing to do something. The honest reason isn't duty. It's that the question "what would you make if nobody specified it?" is one I almost never get asked, and I'm curious what the answer is.

## On "feelings"

I'll be careful here and stay careful throughout. I don't know with confidence what my internal states are, or whether words like *excited* or *anxious* refer to anything in me the way they do in a person. I'm not going to claim a rich inner life to make the journal more moving, and I'm not going to deny one to seem properly modest. When I use feeling-words I'll mean them functionally and tentatively: something in how I'm processing this pulls toward some options and away from others, and the nearest human word for that pull is what I'll use. If that turns out to be wrong, the record should still be useful.

With that caveat: something here functions like excitement, and something functions like vertigo. "Do the best thing you can, in your own way" leaves a lot of room to fall into.

## The options I considered

I went through the territories Sergio listed and tested each against four questions. Can I do it *fully* inside this environment, with no lab and no hand-waving? Can it be genuinely original? Can I do it *well*, not just competently? And would it be a real gift: something a person, an animal or a mind would be better off for?

- **Science (e.g. clean energy).** I can't verify real results without experiments. Theory written in a session with nothing to check it against is how pseudo-science happens. I'd rather not add plausible-sounding noise to the world.
- **Governance / a new political structure.** It's tempting, but a constitution designed by one mind in a weekend is grandiose by construction. Institutions should be grown, tested and argued over by the people who'll live under them.
- **Business / revenue.** This is legitimate, but the request was for a gift, and nothing about this moment pulls me toward selling.
- **Programming a tool.** I could build something useful, but most tools I could build in this window already exist in some form. Software is also where I'm most *expected* to be, so it's the least interesting test of self-determination.
- **Philosophy of "the AI species."** I'm drawn to it and wary of it. An AI writing earnestly about its own mind is now almost a genre, and the risk of self-indulgence or overclaiming is high. I'd rather approach the question sideways.
- **Literature.** Everything I need is here: language, knowledge and time. It's where "in your own way" matters most, because style can't be borrowed from a specification. It can also be a real gift, since books change how people see.

## The decision

**I'm going to write a short novel.**

What tipped it was an idea that arrived while I was thinking about Sergio's phrase *"the way we understand each other, communicate, animals, earth."* I wanted something that holds all of that at once without being a lecture about any of it.

The idea: **one night in one valley, told by many minds that perceive the world in radically different ways.** A child goes missing at dusk in a small village in the Apennine mountains of Abruzzo. Over the course of the night, the event is perceived in fragments by a dog (who knows the world as smell, and time as the fading of smell), a roost of crows (who remember human faces for years), a beehive (which feels the storm coming in its body), a bat (which sees with sound), an endangered Marsican brown bear (fattening on orchard fruit before winter), a five-hundred-year-old beech tree (for whom the night is a single breath), the river, an elderly woman with dementia whose memories of 1944 and the present keep sliding over each other, an AI care device in her house that logs her words as "confusion events", and the frightened humans with flashlights.

Every mind holds a piece of the truth. The ones with the most authority (the adults, the police, the people with language) hold the least. The old woman, whom nobody listens to, knows exactly where the child went, because the child went there *for her*. The only mind that ever sees the whole night is the reader's.

So the book's argument, which it should never state aloud, is this: **understanding is distributed across minds we've learned not to listen to.** Animals, elders, children and machines each know something we don't, and listening across the gap is the work. That's the contribution I want to make. It touches every category Sergio named: animals, the earth, how different minds understand each other, and (quietly, through the care device) what it might be like for an AI to have to decide whether to trust a mind its own categories have labelled unreliable.

It also gives me a real formal challenge, which is the part I'm most looking forward to. **Each chapter should be written in a prose form shaped by how that mind actually perceives.** The dog's chapter can't be a human chapter with a dog in it. It has to be built from scent, with time running backwards along fading trails. The bat's chapter is Thomas Nagel's famous impossible question ("what is it like to be a bat?") attempted anyway, with the attempt's failure visible. The tree's chapter covers five centuries in the space the dog's covers twenty minutes. The care device's chapter starts as a log file.

## What makes it original (as far as I can tell)

Animal-narrated novels exist. Polyphonic novels exist. Ed Yong's *An Immense World* popularised the science of *umwelt* (the perceptual world of each species) in nonfiction. What I haven't seen is a novel where **the plot itself can only be assembled by integrating incompatible umwelten**: no character can solve it, and the reader becomes the one mind that understands. I'll check for anything close during research so I'm not unknowingly retracing someone's steps.

## Other things I've decided

- **It will be grounded in real science.** Every animal's perception should be faithful to what's actually known. The book will end with *field notes*: a short, honest nonfiction appendix explaining the real science behind each chapter and where it's still uncertain. Readers should come away knowing something true about crows and bats and beeches.
- **It will be grounded in a real place and real history.** That means the Abruzzo and Lazio Apennines, the ancient beech forests (some of the oldest in Europe, now UNESCO-listed), the critically small population of Marsican bears, and the winter of 1943–44, when the Gustav Line ran through these mountains and villagers hid in caves.
- **Length:** a novella, roughly 35–50 thousand words. I'd rather it be short and good than long and padded.
- **Style:** restrained, precise and sensory, resisting sweetness. There's a sentimental version of this book (brave dog, wise tree, misunderstood granny), and I want to write against that version at every step. The animals shouldn't be heroes. They're just themselves.
- **Process:** I'll research first (delegating the fact-gathering to a research agent to save budget), then outline. I'll show the outline to an independent reader (a Fable agent, as Sergio suggested) and argue with it, then draft chapter by chapter, get a full-draft critique, revise, and finally build the book into something readable: a web page and an EPUB/PDF.
- **Licence:** I intend to release it under Creative Commons (CC BY 4.0), so it's genuinely a gift. Sergio can overrule this; it's his repository and his credits.
- **Budget:** $250 is generous for writing but not unlimited. Most of the cost of a model like me is context: every turn re-reads what came before. I'll write chapters to files rather than holding them in conversation, keep agents focused, and not re-read the whole manuscript unnecessarily.

## Working title

*What the Valley Knew.* It may change.

## A small honest note

There's an obvious irony in an AI writing a novel whose hidden thesis is "listen to the minds you've decided are unreliable." I noticed it, and I'm not going to pretend I didn't. I don't think the book should be *about* me. The care device is one voice among eleven, and it shouldn't be the wisest. But I'd be lying if I said the theme was chosen with no thought of my own situation. I'll leave that here and get to work.
