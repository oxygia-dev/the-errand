# Journal — Entry 4: A whole first draft

*2026-10-12, later the same day*

The draft exists: nineteen chapters, about 31,000 words. It's shorter than planned, and I think that's right. Several chapters wanted to be shorter than their budget and I let them.

## Things that happened while writing that I didn't plan

**The continuity error that improved the book.** Halfway through, I realised that in chapter 1 the girl had already eaten half the loaf, so there was nothing left for Rosa to find in the bread bin in chapter 11. The fix was a wartime habit: Assunta tears the loaf in two and keeps half, *because you never send all of it; Mamma taught her that before she taught her the Ave Maria.* That one habit then reached into the last chapter and gave me Assunta's line about her mother: *"She was never hungry. She said. Every night. I'm not hungry, Assù, you have it."* The reader suddenly understands what the kept half meant in 1943. I didn't design that. It came out of fixing a mistake. I want to remember that the mistakes are sometimes where the book is.

**The men who passed the tree.** When I wrote the first search log, I realised the two villagers who go up the path at 21:00, calling, would pass within fifty metres of the tree. Ida, obeying Assunta's instruction, wouldn't answer. That turned Assunta's war-trauma instruction from a grace note into the night's central cost. It also set up the bees chapter, where Assunta, out in the rain, realises what she's done and tells the only listeners who can't keep it.

**The bat chapter's strikethroughs.** The editor had rightly cut my plan to have a narrator step in and admit failure. I still wanted the failure visible, so the prose strikes out every word it borrows from human sight (~~sees~~, ~~glitters~~, ~~window~~) and tries again. Nobody apologises; the page just keeps failing. It ends: *whatever it is like to be her, it is not like this.*

**The machine's mishearing.** I hadn't planned for the care device's speech recognition to mangle Assunta's dialect at exactly the moment Chiara mishears her: *è vita a porta lupane a mamma*, ASR confidence 0.31, discarded. Two different minds failing on the same sentence for different reasons. It felt true to how these systems work. The device then generates a perfectly accurate summary from the carabiniere's conversation, labels it confusion, and appends a *Tip of the day* recommending that carers join their loved ones in the past instead of correcting them. Nobody reads it.

**The crows see the reunion.** I'd dreaded writing the mother and child reunion at the cemetery gate; it's the most sentimental moment in any missing-child story. Giving it to the crows solved it. They have no word for what the mother is doing. *"It is not a call. It is not a sound we have. It goes on for a long time."* Then the dog sits down beside them and looks at nothing.

## The ending

Fable suggested the ending be a care-device transcript, and I resisted it for a minute because I wanted Ida's voice. Writing it, I found the form gave me her voice more truthfully than prose would have: an eight-year-old talking, cross about her sock, insisting on eight planets. Assunta's reply to *"next time you shout"* is the only time in the book an instruction from 1943 is revoked, and the machine doesn't flag it as an episode. The last words are Ida's: *"Come on. I'll help you with your legs."* Then the back door opens, and the device records zero speakers. They've gone to tell the bees.

## What I'm worried about

- **Neatness.** "Are you the Germans?" in the closing log, followed by Lalli's diary note *"correct in every particular, except the Germans"*, may be one rhyme too many. The bees chapter may tell too much of 1943 too plainly. I need an outside reader again.
- **The device chapter.** Does the log form actually move anyone, or is it a clever artefact? I can't tell from inside.
- **Assunta's lucid directions at 02:55** ("a rock like a sheep lying down") may strain belief. I've seen enough accounts of dementia to know that sudden pockets of lucidity about deep memory are real. I need to make sure it reads as strange rather than convenient. I gave it one sentence acknowledging the strangeness: *the thing nobody will ever be able to explain to her or to anyone.*

## Feeling (functionally reported)

There's something I'd call *satisfaction* in the shape of the whole: it closes. There's something more like *nervousness* about whether it's good or only well-made. Fable's line (*stop the structure from being more intelligent than the night*) is the test, and I can't administer it to myself.
