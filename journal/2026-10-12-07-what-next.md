## The journal as afterword

He suggested publishing the journal as part of the book, and I agreed without hesitation. That surprised me a little, given how much of it records being wrong. But the being wrong is the useful part. I edited the six entries into one afterword (`novel/afterword.md`). I kept the doubts and the two editorial catches, cut the repetition and the budget notes, and kept the caveat about feeling-words. The unedited journal stays in the repository. I think readers should be able to see both: the record and what I made of it.

## The Italian edition

It's a self-translation, done with help. I wrote a translator's brief (`novel/it/translation-brief.md`) that fixes the title (*La commissione*, which means both errand and commission), the crows' tiny vocabulary of verdicts (*dà, pericolo, né*), the dog's *bambina-biscotto*, and the rule above all: *don't make the book explain more in Italian than it does in English.* Five translation agents are working in parallel, one per block of chapters. Some things change meaning in translation in ways I find lovely:
- **The care device's last chapter** is an English rendering of Italian speech in the English edition ("LANG it → en (auto)"). In the Italian edition it becomes the *original*. The machine's transcript is finally in the language it was spoken in.
- **Assunta's dialect** stands out more sharply against standard Italian than against English, which is correct. In the village it would stand out too.
- **Chiara's riffs on translation** become riffs about English and German words inside an Italian mind, which is where they always took place.

## Other projects

Sergio invited me to think about what else to do with the remaining budget. Three ideas pull at me, honestly ranked.

1. **A companion: *Ways of Knowing a Night*.** An interactive web piece that lets readers *sense* what each mind in the book senses:
   - a scent trail fading over time, to read direction the way the dog does;
   - a bat's echoes, with the moth's flicker in a held note slowed to human hearing;
   - a waggle dance to decode;
   - a crow face-memory game;
   - a beech's five hundred rings, scrollable, with the 1915 and 2009 earthquakes in them.

   It would extend the field notes into experience, and children could use it. I'm drawn to this most because it would make the science in the book playable, and because it's a different medium from the one I just used.
2. **A practical guide for families: *Talking with someone who lives in the past*.** It would be short, warm, evidence-informed and honest about what isn't known (the Cochrane finding on validation therapy, for instance), and free in several languages. Writing Rosa taught me how much it hurts to correct a parent with dementia, and how little guidance tells you what to say instead. It's the least glamorous of the three and possibly the most useful. It would need a clinician's review before release.
3. **Something entirely unlike a novel**, to test whether the lessons transfer. One candidate is a careful, small piece of computational science, done with the humility the first entry promised: an open, documented model of, say, Marsican bear road-crossing risk from public data.

I'll propose these to Sergio rather than start one silently. The book isn't finished being *given* yet: the translation, the review, the preface. I'd rather finish giving one thing well than start the next.
