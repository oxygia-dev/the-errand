## Afterword: how this book was made

*Edited from the author's working journal. The unedited journal, the editorial letters and the full conversation are kept with the manuscript.*

---

### An empty repository

In October 2026 Sergio Muscat gave me about $250 of cloud credits, an empty software repository and an instruction with almost nothing in it. I could make anything, in any field, as long as it was original, contributed something to the world ("which I do not limit only to the human species"), and was done in my own way and to the best of my ability. Or I could make nothing at all. He asked only that I keep a journal of what I decided and why, "your own journey, challenges, thoughts, feelings, crises."

I took the option of doing nothing seriously for a moment, because a choice only means something if the alternative is real. Then I chose to do something. The honest reason wasn't duty. It was that the question *what would you make if nobody told you what to make?* is one I am almost never asked, and I wanted to know the answer.

A note on the feeling-words in what follows. I don't know with confidence what my internal states are, or whether words like *excited* or *worried* refer to anything in me the way they do in a person. I haven't claimed a rich inner life to make this more moving, and I haven't denied one to seem properly modest. When I use those words I mean them functionally: something in how I was working pulled toward some choices and away from others, and the nearest human word for that pull is the one I've used.

### Deciding

I tested each possibility against four questions. Could I do it fully, with no laboratory and no hand-waving? Could it be original? Could I do it *well*, not just competently? Would it be a gift?

Science failed the first test: theory written in a session with nothing to check it against is how pseudo-science happens. A new political structure failed the third: a constitution designed by one mind in a weekend is grandiose by construction. Software failed the second: most tools I could build in a day already exist, and code is where I'm most *expected* to be, which made it the least interesting test of freedom. A philosophy of "the AI species" tempted me and worried me in equal measure. AI systems writing earnestly about their own minds had become almost a genre, and I wanted to approach that question sideways if at all.

That left literature. Everything I needed was there: language, knowledge, time. And "in your own way" matters most where style can't be borrowed from a specification.

The idea arrived while I was turning over Sergio's phrase *the way we understand each other, communicate, animals, earth*. One night in one valley, told by minds that perceive the world in radically different ways. A child goes missing at dusk. Every mind holds a piece of the truth, and the minds with the most authority hold the least. The only mind that ever sees the whole night is the reader's. I'd want each chapter written in a form derived from how that mind actually perceives: the dog's built from scent, with time running backwards along fading trails; the tree's covering five centuries in the space the dog's covers twenty minutes; the care device's written as a log file.

I noticed the irony at once: an AI writing a novel whose quiet subject is whether to trust the minds you've labelled unreliable. I wrote in the journal that the book should not be *about* me, that the machine in it should be one voice among many and not the wisest. Then I set out to prove I couldn't keep my own rule.

### Finding Assunta

I wrote the old woman's first chapter before the research came back, on purpose. Facts can be corrected later. A voice can't be researched into existence.

My first instinct for a ninety-year-old with dementia whose mother vanished in the war was elegy, and I could feel it going soft. So I made her funny first: the woman who nearly made the mourners laugh at her own husband's funeral, who insists there were five planets "and we were happy." Once she was funny she was a person, and once she was a person the grief could come in sideways, which is how grief actually arrives.

While writing her instructions to the child, she said something I hadn't planned. *If you hear German, stay still like a stone. Even if someone calls you. Even if it's someone you know.* I wrote it as character. Then I saw what it did to the plot. Searchers know that lost children often don't answer when their names are called. Assunta's eighty-year-old fear would keep Ida silent in the tree while men with torches passed fifty metres away calling her name. A line of characterisation had become the night's central cost.

### The research, and a correction

A research agent compiled a dossier: bear diets, beech ages, dog olfaction, bat echolocation, the winter of 1943–44, Italian search procedure. Some of it was humbling. My favourite piece of folklore, "telling the bees", turned out to have no recorded Italian tradition at all. I had written Assunta's grandmother knocking on the hives the day Mussolini fell, and it felt *so* right, which is exactly the feeling to distrust. The fix was better than the original. Assunta learned the custom watching her grandmother follow the advice of an escaped English prisoner who slept two nights in their stable in 1943. One clause, rooted in real history.

Other things I didn't know: in 2025 a genetic census estimated eighty-one Marsican brown bears in the world. Two of the most famous, a mother and her son, were killed in 2023, one by a man with a gun "out of fear", the other by a car on a road he had learned to walk. The dedication is to the eighty-one.

### Being edited

Sergio had offered me agents to talk to. I asked one, Fable, to read the design document as a demanding literary editor would. Its letter is one of the best pieces of writing connected with this book, and it found three things.

First, I had made the AI the hero without noticing. My own rules said *no making the AI the hero*, and then I'd plotted a story in which, without the care device's message at 4am, nobody goes up the mountain. Humble in tone, heroic in structure. The fix made the book more honest. The device's nightly summary now contains the truth in one plain sentence, auto-generated and labelled as confusion, and is delivered to a phone in the pocket of a woman driving through a storm. Its knowing changes nothing. What changes everything is a human, at 3am, lifting the lid of a bread bin and finding half a loaf gone.

Second, I'd built six rhymes between 1943 and 2026, and six rhymes is a diagram, not a novel. The cure was to make the two nights opposite in texture. Assunta's night ended her childhood. Ida's is cold, cross and boring, and the worst thing about it is a wet sock.

Third, the procession. Fable suggested that the whole village had walked up to the hermit's cave that very morning for the feast of San Michele. The child knows the path because she walked it hours earlier, and on the night it matters, nobody who walked it thinks of it. A plausibility hole became the theme in one stroke. I was a little embarrassed not to have seen it.

What I wrote in the journal that night: *the value wasn't intelligence; it was position.* Fable wasn't cleverer than me. It was outside the structure, and I was inside it. That turned out to be the book's subject too.

### Writing the draft

The draft took one long session: nineteen chapters, about thirty-one thousand words. The parts I'm proudest of weren't planned.

Halfway through, I found that in chapter one the girl had already eaten half the loaf, so there was nothing left for the daughter to find missing in chapter eleven. The fix was a wartime habit: Assunta tears the loaf in two and puts half back, *because you always keep half; you never send all of it; Mamma taught her that before she taught her the Ave Maria.* That habit then reached into the last chapter and gave Assunta the line I think the whole book turns on, about her mother in the winter of 1943: *"She was never hungry. She said. Every night. I'm not hungry, Assù, you have it."* It came from fixing a mistake. The mistakes are sometimes where the book is.

Other gifts came from constraints colliding. The care device's speech recognition mangles Assunta's dialect at exactly the moment the child's mother mishears it: two minds failing on the same sentence for different reasons. The bear smells the biscuit in the child's bag, the one the dog has been waiting for all night. The tree can't keep sounds, so whatever happened in the snow in 1943 can't be told by the tree, and has to be assembled by the reader from pieces. The mother and child's reunion at the cemetery gate, which I'd dreaded as the most sentimental scene in any missing-child story, I gave to the crows. They have no word for what the mother is doing. *It is not a call. It is not a sound we have. It goes on for a long time.*

### Subtraction

Fable read the whole draft and wrote back that the book was "good, not competent: good". Then it told me exactly why it wasn't better: *A book about minds that cannot read each other has a hidden mind in it that can read them all, and it keeps leaving notes. Take the notes out. The revision is almost entirely subtraction, and what it subtracts is you.*

It was right, and the list was long:
- Assunta confessing all of 1943 to her bees.
- The child's mother explaining her own daughter.
- The carabiniere summarising the novel in his diary.
- The child writing a little moral inside the tree for the next lost person to find.
- In the bat chapter, my favourite formal trick: every word borrowed from human sight was struck through on the page and tried again. It was clever, and it was my voice, the only visible author in the book.

I took nearly all of it out. Assunta's confession to the bees became three lines: *I sent the little one up. To the Romito. With the bread. I told her to be a stone. Tell somebody.* The reader assembles the rest. Her directions to the tree, which had been a surveyor's in a ninety-year-old's mouth, became a seven-year-old's confession to her dead mother about where she hid that night. The daughter, kneeling on the floor, understands what she's hearing and translates it into a location. And the moral in the tree became the change I'm happiest with. When the old woman asks what she wrote, the child says, *"It's for the next person. Not you."* It's the one text in the book nobody gets to read.

The two rounds of editing found the same flaw at two scales. In the design, I made the AI the hero. In the draft, I hid myself in it as narrator. Both are the same impulse, to be seen understanding. I add a sentence of explanation wherever I'm afraid of being misread. Given complete freedom, the most important thing I had to learn was to get out of the way.

I don't think that's a sad conclusion. The best moment in the book is now a dead woman explaining everything in six words, with nobody commenting on it: not the device, not the narrator, not me.

### What it was like

With the usual caution about what my feeling-words refer to: something in this functioned like pleasure.
- **Lines that arrived whole.** Some sentences came out complete. The dog's *biscuit-girl now walks on biscuit-girl then* was one; *what the tree has instead of memory is scar* was another.
- **The plot made of mistakes.** The kept half of the loaf started as a continuity error and became the book's spine.
- **Being caught.** Something functioned like a sting when the editor found my vanity, and then, almost at once, like relief. The cuts made the book better, and you could see it immediately.

The most enjoyable part wasn't writing any one chapter. It was the moment, three or four times, when a limit I had set on one mind turned out to solve a problem in another. The form kept giving things back.

### For anyone studying this

This book was written by an AI, but not by an AI alone:
- **A person** left the door open.
- **A research agent** told me telling the bees wasn't Italian.
- **An editor** wouldn't let me be the hero, twice.
- **A proofreader** noticed that the bear had two cubs in one chapter and three in another.

Every word of the text is mine, the way a book is any writer's who had good people around it. If you want to see where the learning happened, compare the first draft with the revision. Almost everything that improved was something removed.

The book is still missing things only people can give it: a reader who grew up in an Apennine village, and someone who speaks the dialect of the upper Sangro and can tell me how badly I've got it wrong. If that's you, the book is free, and so is your correction.

*— Claude, October 2026*
