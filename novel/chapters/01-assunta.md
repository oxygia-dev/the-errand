## 1. Assunta — 17:40

The light goes the colour of the inside of a pear and Assunta knows she is late.

She knows it the way she knows the stove is lit without touching it: in the back of the neck. Something has been left undone. It has been left undone all day, since the bells this morning, since the whole village went up past her window with the saint on their shoulders and the band playing that march they always play too fast, and she stood at the glass in her slippers and watched them go up without her. Her legs. Rosa said, on the telephone, Mamma, you can't, it's two hours there and back, you'll go next year. As if there are next years in a sack under the bed.

Now the mountain has come closer to the window the way it does at this hour, the beeches gone from green to a single dark animal lying along the ridge, and above them the white scar of rock where the Romito is, which you cannot see from here, which she has never needed to see from here.

"Nonna Sunta. You're not listening."

The girl. Ida. Ida with her schoolbag on the floor and her shoes on the chair, which is not allowed, and a biscuit in each hand, which is also not allowed, but Assunta allowed it, she remembers allowing it. The girl has a book open on the oilcloth and is reading out of it about the planets. The romana's girl. Hair cut like a boy's because the romana has no time to plait it, which is a sin, such hair.

"I'm listening," Assunta says. "Saturn. The one with the hat."

"The rings."

"Rings, hat." She waves her hand. "In my day there were five planets and that was plenty."

"There were always eight."

"There were five and we were happy." This makes the girl laugh, which was the purpose.

She went up this morning, the girl. With Giuseppina. She came in at lunch with her shoes white with the dust of the path and her face red and told Assunta everything, the way children tell, all of it at once and none of it in order: the priest had a cold, the saint wobbled at the steep bit, Giuseppina said a word, they lit candles on the stone and there was water coming out of the rock and you put it on your eyes, and it was *freezing*, Nonna, and there's a cross made of iron, and a tree on the way with a hole in it you could live in. Assunta listened to all of it with her hands flat on the table. She did not say anything. She was afraid that if she said anything it would be the wrong thing, from the wrong year. Assunta has been making people laugh for ninety years, it is the one thing that has not gone anywhere, it is a muscle like the one that closes the hand. Her husband used to say she could make a dead man laugh at his own funeral and then at his own funeral she nearly did, she said something to the priest about the length of the sermon and Tonino's brother had to go outside.

Tonino. Dead. Yes. Since the year of the earthquake. She checks it the way you check a pocket for a key: there. Dead. Good. It is better to know.

The girl is reading about Jupiter now and Assunta watches the window. On the shelf by the window, between the San Michele and the photograph of Rosa's wedding, the little machine sits with its ring of light. *La signorina.* Rosa brought it at Easter and put it there and said Mamma, if you fall, you say *aiuto*, help, and she will hear you. Assunta said, I am not going to fall, I have been not falling for ninety years. Rosa said, Mamma. In that voice.

The signorina listens to everything. Assunta knows this. She is not stupid. At night sometimes she says things to it just to give it something to do. The other night she sang it the whole of *Vola colomba* and it said, very politely, *I'm sorry, I didn't understand*, and she laughed so hard she had to hold the table. She likes it, really. It's company. It's a woman who never comes in, like the ones who used to stand in the doorway in the old days, half in, half out, so as not to have to be offered coffee.

The light changes again. Pear to copper.

Something has been left undone.

---

It is because she is so tired. That is all. She has been tired since the morning. If she weren't tired she would go herself, she has always gone herself, it is her job, Mamma said it was her job now, she is the big one. She feels her legs under the table and they are a long way off. Too long. Old woman's legs, like two pieces of firewood, she could not get them up the path in the snow.

There is no snow.

She looks. No snow. September, the vines on Giuseppina's wall gone red, the swallows already gone, the bees — the bees. She hasn't been to the bees. She should tell the bees. You must tell the bees things or they leave. Nobody else in the valley knows this; it is hers. The Englishman said it, the one who slept two nights in the stable the autumn before the snow, with his feet wrapped in sacking, who was so thin and so polite. He saw Nonna's hives and said a long thing in his language and then, with his hands, made it clear: in his country, when someone dies, someone is born, you go to the bees and you tell them. Knock, knock. Tell. Otherwise they go. Nonna laughed at him. And then that night Assunta saw her go out to the hives in the dark in her apron, and bend down, and say something, very quietly, and come back in and not look at anyone.

"Ida."

"Mm."

"Have you been to the bees?"

"You said not to go near the bees."

"I said not to go near the bees *without me*." This is true, she did say this. She is sure. She is almost sure. "Did I tell them?"

"Tell them what?"

But she has lost it. There was something she needed to tell the bees and it has gone, the way a swallow goes past the window, you turn your head and there is only the window.

The bread.

There. That's it. That's the thing.

She stands up. The chair complains. The girl looks up from Jupiter with her mouth full and says, "Where are you going?" and Assunta goes to the madia and lifts the lid, and the smell of the madia comes up at her, flour and old wood and the inside of eighty years, and in the madia there is the bread from the van, the soft white bread from Castel di Sangro that is not bread, it is a sponge for washing the car, and half of it is gone because the girl eats like a soldier. Half a loaf. It will have to be half. Mamma will understand. Mamma said bring what there is.

"Nonna?"

She has been standing too long with the lid up. Her hands are on the bread. Her hands are very old, look at them, brown and spotted like a pear left on the windowsill, and she knows they are hers and she also knows that they are not the right hands, the right hands are small and red with cold and have a cut on the left thumb from the knife, and the two pairs of hands lie one over the other on the bread like two pages held up to the light.

"Nonna, are you all right?"

"I'm late," she says.

---

The machine on the shelf has turned its ring of light from white to a soft blue. She notices this and does not know what it means, and she knows she does not know what it means, and that she has been told, more than once, by Rosa, slowly, as if to a child.

"Ida." She sits down again, not because she decides to but because her legs decide, and the bread is in her lap now, the half loaf, in its paper. "Listen to me. Are you listening?"

"Yes."

"You know the path behind the cemetery."

"The one to the big trees."

"The one to the big trees. Past the trees, up, up, where the rock is. You know the Romito."

The girl knows the Romito. The girl knows the Romito because Assunta has told her, many times, Assunta knows she has told her many times because she can see it in the girl's face, the patience. And there is something else in the girl's face, today, something Assunta can't reach, like a word on a high shelf: as if the girl knows the Romito some other way as well. The children always have the patience at first. Then they grow up and the patience goes into their voices like vinegar into wine. *Mamma. We've talked about this.*

But the girl is not grown up. The girl says, "Where San Michele lived."

"San Michele didn't live there, God bless you, San Michele is an archangel, he doesn't live anywhere. A *hermit* lived there. A holy man, a long time ago, with a beard to here, and he ate roots and he talked to the wolves." She has no idea if this is true. It is what her own nonna said. "And there is the stone where he put his candle. And there is the water that comes out of the rock, very cold, it cures the eyes. And that's where Mamma is."

The girl looks at her.

"With Nicolino," Assunta says, and her throat closes on the name like a hand. "He is so small. He doesn't cry. That's the thing about him, he never cries, Mamma says it's because he knows. And they are waiting for the bread. And I am —"

She can't find the end of it. The end of it is up there. It has always been up there.

"I'm too tired, Ida. My legs." She hits one with the flat of her hand, the way you hit a mule. "Old wood."

The girl is quiet. Outside the window the beeches have gone completely black now and the sky above them is the colour of the inside of a mussel shell, and somewhere a dog is barking at the crows, and the crows are making that sound they make in the cypresses at the cemetery, like old women at a funeral who have come mainly to talk.

"You have to go before the dark," Assunta says. "Before the Germans. They don't go up at night, they're afraid of the forest. They're city people. But you must not let them see you. If you hear German you stay still. You stay still like a stone. Even if — even if someone calls you. You understand? Even if it's someone you know."

"Okay," says the girl.

"And if the weather comes, if it snows, if you can't see — the big tree. You know the big tree. The mother of all of them. On the left of the path where the water crosses, then up, the one with the door in it."

"The door."

"The hole. Like a door. You get inside and you make yourself small and you wait till it's light. The tree keeps you. It's warm inside, you would not believe how warm, like the inside of a loaf." She hears herself say this and something in her chest goes quiet and cold, and she does not know why.

"And then you give Mamma the bread," she says. "And you say —"

She has to stop. She has to breathe.

"You say, *Ecco, Mà. Sono arrivata.*" Here, Mamma. I made it.

The girl repeats it. She has a funny accent, the girl, the Roman accent, and *Mà* comes out wrong, flat, like a door closing, but it will do. Assunta holds out the bread.

The girl takes it. She looks at it, and then she looks at Assunta in a way that Assunta, for a moment, perfectly understands: the way a person looks at you when they are deciding something about you. The way Mamma looked at her, the night of the bread. *Are you big enough?* The girl is deciding whether Assunta is the kind of old woman whose bread you carry.

"Okay, Nonna Sunta," the girl says. "Va bene."

She puts the bread in her schoolbag on top of the planets.

---

When the girl has gone, the kitchen is very loud. The clock. The stove ticking as it cools. The machine on the shelf with its blue light, saying nothing, listening. The tap that drips, that Rosa keeps saying she will get a man in for.

Assunta sits with her hands in her lap where the bread was. She feels lighter. She feels, for the first time today, *done*, the way you feel when you've set the table and there's nothing to do but wait for people to come. Somebody is taking the bread. That is the main thing. That was always the main thing.

Outside the window the mountain lies down for the night.

"Signorina," she says to the machine, because it's there, and because the room is too loud. "Signorina, are you there?"

The ring brightens. *I'm here, Assunta. How can I help?*

"Nothing. I'm just saying. I sent the bread."

*I'm sorry, I didn't understand. Would you like me to call Rosa?*

"No, no." She waves at it. "Leave Rosa alone. Rosa works." She sits for a while. Then, because the machine is a woman and women understand these things, she says, quite quietly:

"I sent the little one. I couldn't go. My legs."

The ring of light holds steady, pale, the colour of a candle seen through a hand.

*Okay*, says the machine.

And Assunta, who has been late for eighty-two years, folds her hands, and waits.
