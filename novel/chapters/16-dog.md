## 16. The dog

The hour of the biscuit.

It is in him the way the hour of the bones is in him, the way the hour of the bins behind the bar on the morning the lorry comes is in him: a hook, low in the belly, that starts to pull before the light. Every morning in the grey, when the doors open and the small ones come out of them with their bags, biscuit-girl comes out of hers and down the street and he is there, at the corner, where he has been since before she came out, and she says the sound, *Biscotto*, and her hand comes down, and in the hand is the yellow one.

Every morning. This is a thing that is true.

The light is not here yet. But the hook is.

---

He has been lying in the doorway across from the old woman's all night with his chin on his paws.

He did not go to the bar. He did not go to the bones. There were no bones; the woman who puts them out was out in the street all night with the others, with the lights, with the noise, and the back door of the bar was shut and smelled only of itself. He lay in the doorway because it was out of the rain and because from the doorway he could smell everything: the old woman's house with the old woman in it, asleep, and then the old woman's daughter, who came in the night smelling of a car and the far city by the sea and fear, and the street, and the men coming and going in the street with their fear, all the men, all night, so much fear on them that the whole village smelled of it, a sour grey fog lying in the streets with the rain.

And the strange dog. Heavy, long-eared, loose-lipped, slow, from a car, from a far place, with a man holding it on a long line. It came up the street in the night with its nose down and went to biscuit-girl's door and then to the old woman's door and then up, up the street, toward the place of the stones, toward the path, following her. He stood up when it passed. Every hair. It did not look at him. It was working. It went up the path into the dark with its man, and a long time later it came back down with its man, and its man was saying *niente* and the strange dog was wet to the shoulders and smelled of the cold green water and of having stopped.

He lay down again. He did not follow it. He knew where it had stopped. He had stopped there too.

---

Now the hook.

He gets up. Shakes. Goes up the street. Past the place of the stones, where a woman is sitting on the wall with a crackling silver thing round her shoulders, and another woman beside her with smoke. He knows the first one. Biscuit-girl's mother. She smells of biscuit-girl, a little, the house-smell, and of salt, and of the fear more than anyone, so thick on her he has to put his ears back going past. She looks at him. She does not say a sound to him. He goes on. On to the path. Up.

The trees. The cold.

The night is all over the path, all of it lying there in layers: the men going up and the men coming down, and the strange dog going up and the strange dog coming down, and blood, a little, a man's, on a stone, and the rain over all of it, flattening it, washing it into the cracks. Under that the morning, the whole village, the blanket, gone thin. Under that biscuit-girl going up yesterday, faint, faint, washed nearly out, a ghost of a ghost. He follows it anyway. It is going the way he is going.

The water.

It is still loud. It is still fast and brown and high on the stones. But it is lower than it was in the night — he can smell where it was, the wet line on the bank above the water where everything smells new-drowned — and there are men at it with a rope, two of them on the far side already, and the men on this side see him and one says *eh, guarda, il cane*, and nobody says *vattenne*.

He does not use the stones. He goes in above them where it is slower, and the cold closes over his shoulders and the water takes him and he swims, hard, crooked, the bank coming toward him sideways, and his feet find it, and he is out, and up, and shaking, and the far bank is all around him.

Big one: old now. Last night's. Washed. Still there, still *no*, the hair on his neck going up by itself and then lying down again, because it is old, because it is going the other way, up, away.

And biscuit-girl.

---

Here is the thing.

On the path she is almost gone. The rain has taken her. But the path is not the only thing. The air is moving. At this hour, before the sun, the air on the mountain comes down: the whole slope breathing out, a slow cold slide of air going downhill through the trees toward the water, carrying with it everything it has passed over. And he stands on the far bank with his nose up and the air comes down to him through the trunks and in it, faint, but not old, not a ghost, not yesterday —

*Now.*

Biscuit-girl now. Warm. Wool and salt and soap and the inside of her shoes and the old woman on her hands still, and wood, and dust, and — 

The yellow one.

He goes.

---

Up the path. Fast. The men behind him calling something, not to him. Up past the first turn and the big grey rock that smells of sheep that have not been here for longer than he has been alive, and the air coming down the slope is thicker here, it is coming from the left, from below the rock, from among the trees. He leaves the path. Down through the dead leaves and the empty husks, straight, nose up, not casting, not stopping, the smell getting louder and louder until it is the only thing, until it is a wall, until it is a tree.

A big one. Many arms. A hole at the bottom.

The smell is coming out of the hole.

He puts his head in.

Dark, and dry, and the red dust, and her. Right there. Folded up in the bottom of the hole like a cat in a box, her eyes open, very still. Very still. More still than a live thing. She smells of the night and of cold and of having been afraid for a long time and then of being too tired to be afraid. She smells of the biscuit.

He makes a sound. He doesn't decide to make it.

She says: *Biscotto.*

The come-sound.

His tail goes. It hits the tree. He can't stop it. He pushes his head further in, his shoulders, and she puts her hands on his face, both hands, cold, and pushes, and he backs out, and she comes out after him, all knees and elbows, into the grey, and stands up, and staggers, and puts her arms round his neck and her face into the wet of his neck, and holds on, hard, the way nobody holds on to him, and he stands there and lets her, because she is holding on, because her hands are there.

Then she lets go and takes the bag off her back.

He knows this. He sits. He doesn't need to be told to sit; he sits the way he sits at the corner every morning, square, with his front feet together, looking at the bag.

She opens it. She takes out the yellow one.

"I saved it," she says. "It's yours."

He takes it from her hand very gently, the way he always does, and eats it.

Behind them, coming down through the trees, the men, loud, and one of them shouting a sound, again and again, and the sound breaking in half. Biscuit-girl turns her head toward them. She looks at the men coming. She looks at him. She puts her hand on his head and leaves it there.

He finishes the biscuit and looks at her hand to see if there is another one. There isn't.

It is morning. That is the hour. The hook in his belly, which has been pulling all night, goes slack.
