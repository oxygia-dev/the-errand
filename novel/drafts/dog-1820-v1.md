## The dog — 18:20

Biscuit-girl.

Biscuit-girl going up. Her smell laid down on the stones of the path in small warm clouds, one, two, one, two, each cloud a little stronger than the one behind it, so that the path itself says *this way, this way, she is this way* — the old clouds faint as yesterday, the new clouds loud as now. He follows the loud. Nose down. The world comes in through the front and goes out through the sides, in, in, in, out, the air turning over and over in him like a stone in a stream, and every turn of it is full.

Here is what is on the path.

Biscuit-girl: sweet and salt and the inside of a shoe, and soap, the one with flowers, and the biscuit, the yellow one, in the bag on her back where it always is. Under that, the bread. New today. White bread, the van-bread, that smells of the van. Under that, something else, sharp, a little sour — the smell biscuit-girl makes when she is doing something big. Not fear. The one before fear. The one that comes off the young ones of men when they climb a wall.

The old woman: on the bread, on biscuit-girl's hands. The old woman is dust and wax and the yellow from the boxes that hum and something underneath, under everything, a smell like the inside of a cupboard that has had the same thing in it for a very long time.

Fox: yesterday. Going across. Ignore.

Men: three days, four days. The one with the brown hat. The one with the saw. Boots. Petrol. Ignore.

Hedgehog. Old. Ignore.

Cat, the grey one from the bar. Today. Arrogant. Ignore, ignore — no — no, ignore.

Biscuit-girl.

---

He has two sounds that mean him.

One is *vattenne*. The men say it, and the women at the bar, and the old man with the blue gate who smells of oil and iron and the thing that went off in the winter. *Vattenne* comes with a boot, or a stone, or a hand going up. It means him. It means *now go*.

The other sound is *Biscotto*. Only biscuit-girl says it. It comes with a hand coming down. It means him too. It means *now come*.

He came.

---

The trees begin. The path goes into them and the air changes all at once, as if he has put his head into a hole: cold, wet, deep, every smell lying low and close and slow under the leaves, the earth breathing up through the dead leaves in a breath so old and so big that for a moment he stops and stands in it with his mouth open, tasting, the roof of his mouth tasting, mushroom and rot and root and the small quick deer and the beetle and the long story of everything that has died here and gone back into the ground, which is everything. It is a lot. It is always a lot. He lets it go and finds her again.

Biscuit-girl. Up.

She is ahead; not far. He can hear her, the scuff, the breath. She is talking. Biscuit-girl talks when she walks; she talks to him, and to the birds, and to nothing. Now it is to nothing: the same sounds, over and over, quick and low.

*Ecco, Mà. Sono arrivata. Ecco, Mà. Sono arrivata.*

He catches her up. He puts his nose in the back of her knee and she says, "Biscotto! You scared me," and her hand comes down and is on his head and the hand smells of the bread and the old woman and herself, and he leans his whole side against her leg the way he does, the way that makes her stagger and laugh, and she staggers and doesn't laugh.

"You can come," she says. "But you have to be quiet. In case of the Germans."

He does not know this sound. He watches her mouth. He waits for the biscuit. There is no biscuit. She walks on.

He walks on.

---

The water.

He hears it long before. Smells it longer: the cold green smell that comes up off the stones where the water goes over them fast and white. The path goes down to it and across it on the flat stones and up the other side.

Biscuit-girl goes down the bank. Her smell goes down in front of her, down, to the edge.

He follows her to the edge. He puts his front feet in. Cold. Fast. The water takes everything and runs away with it downhill; the water is a mouth that eats smells; at the edge of it the smell of biscuit-girl stops as if it had been bitten off. She is across. She is on the stones in the middle, arms out, wobbling. She is on the far side. Her red thing has come off her head — no — she has it in her hand — no —

There. In the water. Red. Going.

Her smell goes with it, a thread of it, a little red thread of her smell going away down the water very fast, and she says a sound, a small high sound, and stands on the far bank looking at the water, and then looks up the hill, and then at the water, and then she turns and goes on up. Leaving it. Leaving the red. She is bigger than she was this morning; she has decided something.

He puts his nose up to cross.

And stops.

---

On the far side. On the far bank. Up the far bank where the earth is soft and black and churned. Over everything; over the water-smell and the leaf-smell and the deer and the fox and even over biscuit-girl, *through* her, as if she had walked into it and come out wearing it —

Big one.

Not yesterday. *Now*. Now-now. The smell of it so new it is still warm in the air, a great hot dark wall of smell, musk and fruit and earth and meat-breath and fur, fur, more fur than anything, and milk — milk — and the small ones, two, the sharp new smell of two small ones close to her —

His body does it before he does. He is back. He is three steps back up the near bank with every hair from his neck to his tail standing up and his lip off his teeth and a sound coming out of the bottom of him that he did not decide to make. He knows this smell. Not with his nose; lower. The smell knows him. The smell is older than he is; it is in the back of his throat from before he was born; a long line of dogs going back and back behind him all saying the one thing about it, which is *no*.

He barks. He barks it at the far bank. At the dark. At biscuit-girl, who is up the bank now, who is going on up into it, into the big wall of the smell, small and bright in it like a crust in a mouth. He barks *no, no, no, no*.

She looks back. Once. Across the water and the dark.

"Shh," she says. "Biscotto. *Shh.* Go home."

*Go home*. He knows that one. That one isn't a boot. That one is just a sound.

He barks again. She goes up. The dark closes over her and over the smell of her and the smell of the big one closes over both.

He stands on the near bank with his feet in the cold, with his whole body pointing, quivering, and the water going past him downhill full of her, a thread of her, red, going.

It is getting dark. In the dark the big one is bigger.

At the bottom of the hill, in the yellow, there is the bar, and the back door of the bar, and the woman who puts out the bones at the back door when the light goes, and the bones, the smell of the bones is in him, the hour of the bones is in him like a hook.

He looks up the hill one more time.

Then he turns and runs down, down, with the water running beside him, the two of them running the same way, both of them full of her.
