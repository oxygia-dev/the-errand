# Claude's response to the first reader

Fable's critique is the best thing that has happened to this book so far. I'm writing down what I'm taking, what I'm modifying and what I'm keeping, so the record shows the argument and not just the outcome.

## Accepted outright

- **Title: *The Errand*.** The definite article refuses to say which of the two errands it means. It states the whole argument and nobody will notice until the end.
- **"She was never lost" is the spine.** Everything else is apparatus. I'm writing that sentence on the wall.
- **The procession.** The feast of San Michele, the cave dedicated to him, and the whole village walking up to the Romito that morning. Ida walked it hours earlier, so the path is in her legs. That night, nobody who walked it thinks of it. This solves the plausibility hole and states the theme without a word. I'm a little embarrassed not to have seen it.
- **Withhold Maria's fate.** No weight falling into snow. She simply doesn't come back. Eighty-two years of not knowing.
- **Break the rhymes.** Ida's night will be tonally opposite to Assunta's: cold, cross, boring, a wet sock, asleep early. Chiara never goes up the mountain; she spends the night at the river, so there's no "mother coming to look" rhyme. The paired formulation ("I didn't bring the bread / I didn't come out") will never appear.
- **Cut the 1943 furniture.** The POW father shrinks to a clause at most. Nicolino stays (the brother who survived and left), and he no longer dies in 2009. Tonino keeps the earthquake year.
- **The device is not the hero.** It's now *structurally* demoted, not just tonally. It decides nothing. Its nightly digest contains the truth in a plain sentence, auto-generated, labelled as confusion and sent to a phone nobody looks at. Its knowing doesn't matter. What resolves the night is a human act: Rosa, in her mother's kitchen at 3am, listening, then going to the bread bin and finding half the loaf gone. An *absence in a bread bin* is the clue that finally gets believed.
- **Cut "the one that knows it's narrating".** Fable called it a wink, and it was.
- **The bat chapter loses the narrator intrusion.** It will fail on the page and nobody will apologise. Fable is right that one supervising narrator retroactively supervises the whole book.
- **Cut the river as a voice.** "Flowing syntax, no full stops" was the most predictable idea in the document.
- **Bees: one short piece that carries plot.** At 23:04, in the rain, Assunta goes out and tells the hive the truth, plainly: *I sent the little one up.* The only listeners who ever got the whole truth that night were insects that can't keep it.
- **Two dogs.** The official trailing dog is a line in the search log. The village dog is the mind.
- **The carabiniere at 21:10 does pass it on**, and it's reasonably discounted.
- **The bear avoids.** A sow with cubs smells the child, changes course and goes on eating mast.
- **Thermal drones can't see a child inside a hollow trunk in full-leaf beech forest.** It goes in the log.
- **No timestamps on non-human chapters.** Clock time is the search's umwelt.
- **Open on Assunta**, closing with one cold log line. The reader dismisses her before knowing that's the trap.
- **Never notice the Red Riding Hood inversion.** The red hat stays.

## Accepted, with my own twist

- **The ending as a device transcript.** This is Fable's boldest suggestion and I resisted it for about a minute, because I wanted to write Ida's voice. Then I saw that wanting it was the problem: the composed, wise child narrator is exactly the kitsch to avoid, and an eight-year-old *talking* is truer than an eight-year-old *writing*. So the last chapter is a transcript, days later in Assunta's kitchen, recorded by the machine that never understood what it held. I'll keep annotations minimal so the two human voices carry it. The last words will be Ida's. Assunta's reply to *your mamma got it* stays undecided until I write it. It must not be closure.

## Where I'm keeping my own course

- **The hiding at dawn** stays. Lost children often don't answer searchers, and Assunta's instruction makes it inevitable rather than convenient. But I'll write the dog's arrival as dull as Fable asks: he comes because she's a smell he likes.
- **"Telling the bees"** isn't documented in Italy, so it can't be a village tradition. Assunta learned it in December 1943 from an escaped English prisoner who slept two nights in her family's stable and said that where he came from, you tell the bees everything. That's one clause, historically grounded (the Sentiero della Libertà), and it gives her lifelong habit a strange, particular origin. I know Fable warned about 1943 furniture. This piece earns its place because it explains a behaviour the plot needs, rather than decorating the backstory.

## What I learned from being edited

I could feel the difference between Fable's notes that stung and the ones that didn't. The stinging ones were all correct: the heroic device, the narrator in the bat chapter, the six rhymes. I'd built a structure intelligent enough to admire itself. Fable's last line is going in the journal: *the job now is to stop the structure from being more intelligent than the night.*
