## 1. What is happening

### 1.1 Why recent memory fails first
- Ribot (1881) proposed a time gradient in retrograde amnesia: recent memories are more likely lost than remote ones. [V] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ribot%27s_law
- In Alzheimer's disease a gradient is found, but it is **shallower and not consistent**. [V] Same Wikipedia page; also https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3584298/ (listed in results)
- A competing explanation: how often a memory has been retrieved and rehearsed may matter more than its age. One study of mild cognitive impairment (aMCI) and early AD concluded that retrograde amnesia extent was "mainly mediated by the frequency of memory retrieval". [V] https://content.iospress.com/articles/journal-of-alzheimers-disease/jad150722 (as summarised; I did not confirm the exact paper)
- Some apparent gradients may be test artefacts (item difficulty, floor effects). [V] same search
- Mechanism in plain terms: early AD damages medial temporal structures (hippocampus, entorhinal cortex) needed to lay down new memories. Older, well-rehearsed, emotionally loaded memories are held more diffusely and survive longer. [B] The guide can say "newer memories are lost first, older ones often last longer, but not always, and not for every person."
- The "reminiscence bump" (autobiographical memories cluster around ages ~10-30) is well documented in healthy people. There is accumulating evidence it also appears in AD. [V] https://psyche.co/ideas/earlier-memories-are-relatively-spared-in-dementia-why and the 2025 Lee & James time-shifting paper (see 1.2)

### 1.2 "Time-shifting" / "living in the past"
- "Time-shifting" is the research term for a person with AD behaving or speaking as if they were in an earlier period of life. [V] Lee & James 2025, "The Time Machine Revisited: an updated account of time-shifting in Alzheimer's disease": https://www.ebsco.com/articles/consumer-health/c55bb915-d6f0-5bee-9e1f-263e71ab9bfa/the-time-machine-revisited-an-updated-account-of-time-shifting-in-alzheimer-s-disease
  - The original "Time Machine" model links it to hippocampal atrophy. The 2025 update (Time Machine 2.0) points instead to disrupted default-mode-network connectivity and a shift from episodic to semantic memory. The authors say it is specific to AD. [V, as summarised]
- Alzheimer's Society (UK) says the wish to "go home" is often caused by time-shifting or general confusion, and can happen at any stage. [V] https://alzheimers.org.uk/blog/i-want-go-home-what-to-say-to-someone-in-dementia-care
- Do not over-claim. I found **no direct experimental study of time-shifting episodes in everyday life**, and the mechanism is still debated. [V that only abstracts and summaries were found; the debate itself is real]
- The guide should present it as: "the person's sense of *now* has slipped back, and their feelings are real in the present." That wording is mine [B].

### 1.3 Confabulation vs lying
- Confabulation: fabricated or distorted memories produced **without intent to deceive**. The person sincerely believes them and is usually unaware they are inaccurate. [V] https://alzda.ca/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/Understanding-Dementia-Confabulation.pdf ; https://www.helpinghandshomecare.co.uk/?p=193431 ; https://psychcentral.com/health/confabulation-definition ("honest lying")
- Lying is a conscious act meant to obscure the truth. [V] Same sources.
- Associated with frontal and medial temporal damage. [V] https://www.medicalnewstoday.com/articles/confabulation-dementia (lower-grade source)
- A distinction worth keeping (my framing): *time-shifting* (sense of the date or life stage has slipped) is not identical to *confabulation* (invented or distorted content). They can overlap. Delusions, hallucinations and misidentification (e.g. mistaking a daughter for a mother) are further separate phenomena. [B]
- Practical point: accusing the person of lying, or insisting "that didn't happen", is advised against. [V] helpinghandshomecare link above

### 1.4 Sundowning
- Definition: increased confusion, anxiety, agitation and pacing in late afternoon or evening, sometimes into the night. [V] Alzheimer's Association tip sheet https://alz.org/getmedia/d929574d-96ab-4d27-9da9-1224cbc19233/alzheimers-dementia-sundowning-ts.pdf (via search summary)
- **Prevalence is genuinely uncertain.** A 2016 review reports a range of 2.5% to 66% depending on setting and definition. [V] https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fmed.2016.00073/full ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5187352/
  - A systematic review of 12 studies gave 2.3-88%. [V] https://www.turkiyeklinikleri.com/article/en-sundowning-syndrome-in-elderly-a-systematic-review-87832.html
  - A conservative "about 20%" is also quoted; it is attributed to Alzheimer's Association data and a National Geographic piece. [V, weak] https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/sundowning-syndrome-symptoms-causes-treatments
  - **No standardised diagnostic criteria; not in DSM-5.** [V] PMC5187352
  - Recommendation for the guide: avoid a single percentage, or say "estimates range widely, from a few percent to a majority, depending on how it is defined."
- **Causes are not established.** Proposed: body-clock (circadian) disruption, tiredness at day's end, unmet needs (hunger, thirst, pain, toilet), low mood, boredom, dim light and shadows, over-stimulation. [V] Alzheimer's Association/NIH tip sheets as summarised: https://www.alzoc.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/07/2022-FINAL-NIH-Tips-for-Coping-with-Sundowning.pdf ; PMC9324910 (light as a risk factor)
- **Evidence for interventions is weak:**
  - Bright-light therapy: a 2024 systematic review found only two RCTs of 10,000 lux lamps, with no significant benefit on sundowning. A 2014 Cochrane review found insufficient evidence for light therapy in dementia generally. [V] https://neurology-asia.org/system/index.php/neuro/article/view/1620 (as summarised)
  - Melatonin evidence is conflicting; no established guidelines for sundowning. [V] same search
- Commonly advised measures, which are **consensus, not trial-proven**: [V as advice in the tip sheets above]
  - Keep a regular routine.
  - Do tiring tasks (bathing, appointments) in the morning or early afternoon.
  - Get daylight and activity in the day, limit long daytime naps.
  - Larger lunch, lighter evening meal.
  - Reduce evening noise and stimulation (TV, chores).
  - Keep rooms well lit in the evening.
  - Check pain, hunger, toilet needs first.
  - Stay calm, do not argue, do not restrain.
  - Medication only on a doctor's advice.
- Caution: caffeine and alcohol reduction, and the idea that closing curtains at dusk helps, are commonly repeated [B], but I did not verify them from a primary source.

## 2. Approaches and evidence

### 2.1 Reality orientation (RO)
- Cochrane review "Reality orientation for dementia", Spector, Orrell, Davies, Woods (2000, DOI 10.1002/14651858.CD001119). Concluded there was **some evidence of benefit on cognition and behaviour**. [V] https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD001119.pub2/information
- **That review has been withdrawn** (the withdrawal note is dated 2003; one listing disagrees on status). It was folded into the broader cognitive stimulation review (CD005562). [V] same search; cochrane.altmetric.com/details/3515625
- So you cannot cite it as current evidence. UCL authors (Orrell and colleagues) said large, well-designed multicentre trials were needed. [V]
- Important distinction (widely made): classroom-style RO, which means correcting a person's errors repeatedly, is different from gentle orientation cues (clocks, calendars, signage). The first can cause distress when the person cannot take the information in. [B]; a caregiver guide notes research on RO "has produced mixed results" [V weak] https://prod.caregivers.com/articles/parent-with-dementia-talks-to-dead-family-members-141131.htm
- Honest line for the guide: *forcing* a person to accept the "real" date or a death, by repeated correction, is not supported as helpful; gentle environmental cues may help some people. [B/?]

### 2.2 Validation therapy (Naomi Feil)
- Developed by Feil between about 1963 and 1980. Based on accepting "the reality and personal truth of another's experience". [V] https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD001394_no-new-evidence-efficacy-validation-therapy-people-dementia-or-cognitive-impairment-has-been
- Cochrane: Neal & Barton Wright, "Validation therapy for dementia" (2003; updated 2010 with no change in conclusions). Authors' conclusion: **insufficient evidence from RCTs** to say whether it works. [V] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12917907/
  - 3 trials included, not poolable. Two isolated significant results (behaviour at 6 weeks vs usual care, Peoples 1982; depression at 12 months vs social contact, Toseland 1997). No significant differences in other comparisons. Searches ran to 2005. [V]
  - Researchers have also challenged the evidence behind some of Feil's core beliefs and her four-stage model (Mal-orientation, Time Confusion, Repetitive Motion, "Vegetation"). Feil argues strongly for effectiveness. [V, as summarised]
- It is therefore "plausible, widely taught, and **not proven**". The guide should not say "validation therapy works."
- Distinction (my framing): *validation as a communication attitude* (acknowledge feelings, don't argue) is mainstream and is not the same as the formal, staged Feil method. [B]

### 2.3 Person-centred care (Kitwood)
- Tom Kitwood, *Dementia Reconsidered* (1997). Aim: maintain and nurture **personhood**. "Malignant social psychology" lists ways others undermine personhood: disempowerment, invalidation, banishment, ignoring, withholding, disruption, disparagement, and others. [V] https://ijpcm.org/index.php/ijpcm/article/view/1114/714 (as summarised)
- The five needs (comfort, attachment, inclusion, occupation, identity) are standard attributions to Kitwood. [B] The search did not confirm the list.
- The practical framework VIPS (Valuing people, Individuals, Perspective of the person, Social environment; Brooker 2007). [V] same search
- One secondary source says Kitwood (1997) regarded lying as part of a harmful social psychology. [V weak, via SingHealth symposium page https://www.singhealthacademy.edu.sg/snc2023/symposium-communicating-with-people-with-dementia-does-lie-telling-support-personhood]. Check Kitwood directly before attributing a position to him.
- Note: person-centred care is a philosophy with some supporting evidence on wellbeing, not a single tested technique. [B]

### 2.4 "Therapeutic lying" / "love lies" and the ethics
**UK Mental Health Foundation, "What is truth? An inquiry about truth and lying in dementia care"**
- Commissioned 2014, funded by the Joseph Rowntree Foundation, published **December 2016**. The panel included people living with dementia and family carers and took evidence from experts. [V] https://www.mentalhealth.org.uk/explore-mental-health/publications/what-truth-inquiry-about-truth-and-lying-dementia-care ; PDF https://www.mentalhealth.org.uk/sites/default/files/2022-09/MHF-dementia-truth-inquiry-report.pdf (URL listed in results; contents not read by me)
- As described by secondary sources: a **continuum** from whole truth to lying, with five typical responses to difficult questions: (1) tell the whole truth, (2) look for another meaning, (3) distract kindly, (4) go along with the person's reality, (5) lie. Start as close to full truth as possible, always with respect and kindness. Use outright untruths only as a **last resort** to prevent unnecessary distress or real harm. [V, secondary] https://housinglin.org.uk/finder.cfm?i=10431 ; https://research-information.bris.ac.uk/ws/files/282695961/bioe.12923.pdf ; https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13607863.2023.2202632
- "Looking for the emotional truth" is how the user phrased it. I did not confirm that exact phrase in the report. [?] The MHF blog "Is it wrong to lie to a person with dementia?" may carry it: https://www.mentalhealth.org.uk/explore-mental-health/blogs/it-wrong-lie-person-dementia (not read)
- Critique: terms like truth, lying, deception are loosely defined in the report; lying is described as "where a carer initiates purposeful deception". [V, secondary]
- Bioethics commentary (Hughes 2021, *Bioethics*) argues there is a category of "conforming to the reality", which is not strictly lying. [V] https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/bioe.12923

**Alzheimer's Society (UK)**
- Page: "Advice for someone worried about bending the truth to avoid unnecessary distress for a person with dementia" (Dementia Together magazine). [V] https://www.alzheimers.org.uk/get-support/publications-and-factsheets/dementia-together/advice-worried-bending-truth-unnecessary-distress-dementia
  - Gist (as summarised): a truthful reply can be hard when someone asks a difficult question or tells a confabulated memory. The Society advises understanding the behaviour first, then deciding when it may be better to lie or not tell the whole truth. The exact wording was not read. [?]
- Its Talking Point forum includes carers who say things like "can't come to the phone"; those are anecdotes, not guidance. [V]

**Views of people with dementia and carers**
- Casey et al. 2019/2020, *Dementia*: 14 people with memory problems and 18 unpaid carers in focus groups. Deliberate deception meant to harm was rejected. White lies were accepted **only if** the carer knew the person well, acted with empathy, and intended only to reduce distress. Distraction or avoidance was preferred to outright lying. Carers feared lies could erode trust. [V] https://doi.org/10.1177/1471301219831525 ; https://www.publichealth.ie/sites/default/files/resources/Should%20I%20Lie%20-%20Approaches%20to%20dementia%20care_Summary_0.pdf
- Murray et al. 2025: ethnographic study proposing a "taxonomy of lies" for moderate-to-severe dementia. [V title only] https://doi.org/10.1177/09697330241246087
- Other analyses ask whether therapeutic lying contradicts person-centred care (Tandfonline 2023) and whether psychologists should teach it (Cambridge, *Behavioural and Cognitive Psychotherapy*). [V titles]
- **I found no trial comparing truthful, validating and deceptive responses on outcomes.** [?] Say so.
- Counter-concerns (from the ethics literature): [B/V mixed] loss of trust if discovered; the carer's own moral distress; staff lying for convenience; the need to keep lies from creating physical risk; and the point that **most "dilemmas" can be handled with options 2-4 without a lie**.

### 2.5 NICE NG97 (2018) "Dementia: assessment, management and support for people living with dementia and their carers"
- NICE says it last reviewed it on 24 October 2025 and the recommendations will not be updated for now. [V] https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng97 (via search summary; confirm)
- **Distress and "non-cognitive symptoms":** do a structured assessment first, covering pain, delirium, unmet needs and environmental causes; use non-drug approaches first, matched to preference and monitored. [V] https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9292575 ; https://www.iatrox.com/q/69f2f451e09ff9bd00cab430/bpsd (secondary)
- **Antipsychotics:** offer only if the person is at risk of harming themselves or others, or has severe distress from agitation, hallucinations or delusions. Lowest effective dose, shortest time, reassess at least every 6 weeks; special caution in Lewy body and Parkinson's disease dementia. [V] same sources; antipsychotic recommendations cited as NG97 1.7.1-1.7.8 [V] https://www.nice.org.uk/indicators/ind12-dementia-anti-psychotic-prescriptions/IND12.pdf
- **Cognitive stimulation:** offer group cognitive stimulation therapy to people with mild to moderate dementia; "consider" group reminiscence therapy. [V, secondary] https://www.nice.org.uk:443/sharedlearning/running-cognitive-stimulation-therapy-cst-groups-as-part-of-core-community-mental-health-team-cmht-work ; https://www.governmentevents.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/Jane-Moore.pdf
- Carer support is section 1.11. [V] I could not confirm specific wording. NICE does **not**, as far as I could find, rule on "therapeutic lying". [?]

### 2.6 Cognitive stimulation therapy (CST)
- Cochrane (Woods et al., 2023 update, CD005562.pub3): 37 RCTs, 2,766 participants, mostly group sessions. Moderate-quality evidence of a **small, short-term cognitive benefit** in mild-to-moderate dementia (MMSE difference 1.99 points, 95% CI 1.24-2.74). Possible gains in communication, mood, quality of life and behaviour. Gap: long-term programmes. [V] https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD005562.pub3/pdf/CDSR/CD005562/CD005562_abstract.pdf ; https://www.alzheimer-europe.org/news/cochrane-review-analyses-studies-cognitive-stimulation-finding-small-benefits-people-mild
- Typical format: ~14 themed sessions over 7 weeks. [V]
- Relevance: CST is a **group programme for mild-to-moderate dementia**, delivered by trained staff. It is not a family conversation technique. Note it is partly "reality-orientation-descended" but non-confrontational. [B]

### 2.7 Alzheimer's Association (US)
- Repetition page: the person may not remember asking; repetition can express a concern, need, anxiety or frustration; look for patterns by time, place or person. [V] https://www.alz.org/care/alzheimers-dementia-repetition.asp
- I did **not** find the Association's own guidance on deceased relatives. Do not attribute that advice to it. [?] The "enter their reality / don't argue / respond to the feeling" line appears across US sources (Wexner Medical Center, Virginia Dept for Aging), but some widely shared lists are by volunteers, not official. [V] https://wexnermedical.osu.edu/our-stories/how-to-communicate-with-someone-with-dementia ; https://www.alzheimersblog.org/?p=2163

## 3. Practical communication (areas of broad consensus)
Basis: the consensus is among major charities and clinical bodies, **not RCT-tested**. Where I could confirm a source it is tagged.

**General principles**
- Don't argue, quiz ("Don't you remember?") or correct. [V] Virginia Dept for Aging https://vda.virginia.gov/downloads/demserv/Caregiver-Tip-Sheets-Repeating%20EN.pdf ; Wexner (above)
- Respond to the **feeling**, not the factual accuracy. [V] Island Health sheet https://www.islandhealth.ca/sites/default/files/2018-04/caregivers-tips-when-communication-difficult.pdf
- Use short sentences, one idea at a time, calm tone, eye level, give time; offer choices ("fish or chicken?") rather than open questions. [V weak] sunriseseniorliving / klarity summaries; also [B]
- Reminiscence and photos are a gentle bridge. [V] Alzheimer's Society "go home" blog

**When they ask for a dead parent or spouse**
- Ask about the person: "Tell me about your mother. What was she like?" Validate the feeling ("you miss her"). [V weak] caregivers.com and syndicated Aging Untold piece (May 2026) https://www.ktiv.com/2026/05/19/how-respond-when-dementia-patients-ask-about-deceased-loved-ones/
- Tell-or-not is the real dilemma. Telling a bereaved person again can cause fresh grief, since each reminder "can feel like the first time" (gerontologist Sam Cradduck, same piece). [V weak]
- Use the MHF spectrum: first try 2-4 (find the meaning; distract; stay in their frame without stating a falsehood: "She isn't here right now" is literally true) before a direct lie. [V secondary + my example, B]
- Experts quoted in that piece say "at work / coming home soon" is acceptable if it prevents distress. [V weak] This sits on the contested end. Flag this as contested.
- If it helps, try to avoid giving **specific false promises** ("she'll be here at 5"), which set up repeated disappointment. [B]
- If you are too upset to redirect calmly, step out and get support. [V weak] same piece

**When they want to go "home"**
- Don't argue about where they are. "Home" may mean comfort or safety, or a place from long ago. Ask them to describe it and why it was happy. Reassure (voice, touch if welcome). Look for loneliness, fear, pain, boredom. Redirect with photos or an activity. [V] https://alzheimers.org.uk/blog/i-want-go-home-what-to-say-to-someone-in-dementia-care
- Walking with them, then turning back after a short stretch, is widely suggested. [B] Make sure they cannot slip away, see section 4.

**When they insist on an old duty (collect the children, go to work)**
- Look for the need underneath: purpose, usefulness, worry about a child's safety. [B]
- Acknowledge ("You've always looked after them"), offer a related safe task ("Let's get their tea ready"), ask about the children. [B]
- Do not simply say "they're grown up, you're 85" (blunt correction) unless gentle versions have failed and the person is calm and able to take it in. [B]
- Never let an old duty become a reason to drive, cook unsupervised or leave the house alone. [B]

**Repeated questions**
- It likely feels like the first time to them. Answer calmly, briefly, same words. Write it on a whiteboard or card if that works. Step away for a break if needed. [V] Alzheimer's Association repetition page; Alzheimer's Society/Dementia UK items as summarised
- Look for the anxiety behind it. Occupy hands and attention. [V] Alz Association

**Agitation and sundowning**
- Calm presence, lower stimulation, warm light, drink or snack, music, a simple task. Check pain, toilet, hunger. Don't restrain. [V] see 1.4
- **If it is new, sudden or severe, treat it as a medical question first**. See below.

**When to seek medical help**
- A **sudden** change in confusion, drowsiness, agitation or withdrawal, especially over a day or two, may be **delirium**. UTI is a common cause, but there are many others. Delirium fluctuates through the day. See a doctor as soon as possible. [V] https://www.alzheimers.org.uk/factsheet/528 ; https://www.alzheimers.org.uk/cy/node/23401
- UTIs can cause confusion with no urinary symptoms in older people; clinicians may need urine and blood tests. Early treatment gives the best chance of recovery; some effects can persist. [V] Cedars-Sinai, Feb 2026 https://www.cedars-sinai.org/newsroom/how-urinary-tract-infections-can-trigger-delirium-and-worsen-dementia/
- Other triggers [B]: pain, constipation, new or changed medicines, dehydration, poor sleep, sight or hearing loss, a change of place.
- Emergency: call local emergency number (UK 999 or 111 for non-emergency; EU 112; US 911). Alzheimer's Society says emergencies go to 999 or 111. [V] https://www.alzheimers.org.uk/get-support/dementia-support-line
- Antipsychotic medicines are not first-line and carry risks; see NG97 above.

## 4. Safety and technology

### 4.1 Wandering ("walking about")
- **60% will wander at least once** is the Alzheimer's Association's widely repeated figure. [V as attributed] https://www.ncoa.org/article/understanding-wandering-risks/
- The research is less tidy: prevalence studies range from **11% to 50%**; one study found 40% got lost and 5% repeatedly. [V] https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11485082
- About 20% of community-dwelling and 60% of institutionalised people are reported to wander. [V] https://practicalneurology.com/diseases-diagnoses/alzheimer-disease-dementias/wandering-sundowning-in-dementia/31912/
- Risk rises with severity. [V] same
- "If not found within 24 hours, 50% chance of serious injury or death" is attributed to the Alzheimer's Association and used by MedicAlert, but I could not find the underlying study. [V as attribution; evidence base [?]] https://www.nysenate.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2010/jeffrey-d-klein/alzheimers-association-expands-medic-alert-safe-return
  - Do not print the "15 minutes" claim, which I could not source. [?]
- Advice [B unless noted]:
  - Recent photo; ID bracelet or card; list of places they might go.
  - Know their old routes (old workplace, childhood home), because time-shifters often head for the "past" address.
  - Keep door locks out of their eyeline; avoid shutting them in (legal and safety issues, see 4.3).
  - If missing, call emergency services at once and say the person has dementia.
- UK **Herbert Protocol**: a form completed in advance (medication, mobile numbers, places previously found, photo) that police use if a person goes missing. [V] https://lancashire.police.uk/help-advice/safer-communities/herbert-protocol (local versions vary)
- US: MedicAlert + Alzheimer's Association 24/7 Wandering Support. MedicAlert (Canada) says 91% of cases where it was involved were returned safely (its own data). [V, source interest] https://www.medicalert.ca/dementia-alzheimers
- Italy: I did not verify a national equivalent. A regional AIMA "GPS Alzheimer" project exists (Tuscany leaflet). [V weak] https://blog-agricoltura.regione.toscana.it/documents/10180/22562840/Depliant%20Progetto%20AIMA%20GPS%20Alzheimer.pdf

### 4.2 What technology can and can't do
- **GPS/location devices**: can speed up finding someone. They don't stop wandering, require the device to be worn and charged, and give no help with the underlying need. [B]
- **Evidence is thin**: a 2015 review found no RCT of electronic tracking, with small studies. A 2024 feasibility study had 45 wearer-carer pairs and called for larger studies. [V] https://www.sintef.no/globalassets/sintef-teknologi-og-samfunn/rapporter-sintef-ts/paper-aaate-2015-oderud-persons-with-dementia-and-there-caregivers-using-gps.pdf ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11290024/
- Reviews of assistive technology for people with dementia show a wide variety of devices, heterogeneous methods and small samples (many case studies; most 1-50 participants). [V] https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7857954/ ; https://pure.ulster.ac.uk/en/publications/a-systematic-review-of-electronic-assistive-technology-within-sup-2/
- **Door alerts, bed/chair sensors, fall detectors**: they alert *after* an event; they don't prevent it, and false alarms and alert fatigue are common. I found no high-quality evidence specifically for these. [B; evidence [?]]
- **Voice assistants / reminder devices**: I found **no evidence** in this session. Plausible risks: a voice that tells the person today's date or "your mother died" could upset them; the person may not understand it is a machine. [?]
- Technology doesn't replace human company. The Alzheimer's Society (older coverage) said safer-walking tech must have the person's full consent and be part of a full care package. [V, dated] https://www.digitalhealth.net/?p=32267
- Consent and dignity: Landau & Werner (2012): no one should be coerced; involve the person; ideally decide at diagnosis, jointly. Privacy, autonomy, dignity and data-sharing are the main ethical issues. [V] https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/international-psychogeriatrics/article/abs/ethical-aspects-of-using-gps-for-tracking-people-with-dementia-recommendations-for-practice/07FC3213C03525779593EA1638DE82AC ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11290024/
- Legal: locking the person in, covert monitoring and sedation raise legal questions that differ by country (UK Mental Capacity Act and Deprivation of Liberty Safeguards; Italian "amministratore di sostegno"; GDPR). [B] Not verified. Tell families to ask a professional.

## 5. Carers

- **Global**: WHO says 57 million people lived with dementia in 2021, over 60% in low- and middle-income countries; ~10 million new cases a year; most care is by family. [V] https://www.who.int/mental_health/neurology/dementia/en/ (via results)
- **US (Alzheimer's Association 2025 Facts & Figures)**: nearly 12 million unpaid carers; 19.2 billion hours; ~$413 billion. 59% rated emotional stress as high or very high; 38% physical stress. [V] https://alz.org/news/2025/november-national-family-caregivers-alzheimers-disease-awareness-month
- **UK**: almost 1 million people with dementia (2024), a third undiagnosed; £42 billion annual cost; families bear 63%. Older Alzheimer's Society estimate of ~700,000 unpaid carers (2021). [V] https://www.alzheimers.org.uk/cy/node/41951 (2021 estimate; 2024 figures via press coverage, https://www.hellorayo.co.uk/kiss/uk/news/dementia-cost-42-billion). I could not verify a 2024 carer count. [?]
- **Mental health**: pooled anxiety prevalence in informal dementia carers 32.1% (95% CI 20.6-46.2; 10 studies, high heterogeneity; Kaddour & Kishita 2020). [V] https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/id/eprint/71505/ . I did not retrieve a pooled depression figure. [?]
- **Italy**: ~1.43 million people with dementia (2025), the highest share in the EU at 2.4%, rising to 2.2 million by 2050 (Alzheimer Europe 2025 report, via Il Sole 24 Ore). [V, secondary] https://en.ilsole24ore.com/art/elderly-dementia-italy-1-bitter-place-in-eu-and-2050-we-will-pass-22-million-cases-AITzXu6 ; primary https://www.alzheimer-europe.org/resources/publications/prevalence-dementia-europe-2025 (not opened)
  - Federazione Alzheimer Italia survey figures: 59.3% of Italian carers said the person was not told the diagnosis; 47% of carers no longer accept invitations from friends (World Alzheimer Report 2024). [?] Derived from a search summary of Italian press. Verify before use.
- **Malta**: I found no current prevalence figure. An undated PDF lists 5,301 people (1.26%), which I would not use. [?]
- **Respite care evidence**: Cochrane review (Maayan et al. 2014, 4 trials, 753 participants) found **no evidence of benefit** on carer burden or institutionalisation, with very low-quality, very small and short trials. [V] https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD004396_respite-care-people-dementia-and-their-carers . Read this as "not shown" rather than "doesn't help". Many families describe respite as essential. [B]
- Support groups and psychoeducation: I did not retrieve evidence in this session. [?] NICE NG97 section 1.11 covers carer support. [V section number]
- Practical: carer's own GP, carer assessment (UK: right to a carer's assessment under the Care Act 2014 [B]), day centres, peer groups, helplines (section 6), Admiral Nurses.
- Guilt about lying or about losing patience is common. The guide can say that carers' own wellbeing is part of safe care. [B]

## 7. What is not known / contested (honest list)

1. **No trial shows that lying, going along, or telling the truth is better** for the person's wellbeing or safety. Evidence is qualitative and ethical argument, not outcome trials. [?]
2. **Therapeutic lying is contested**: MHF accepts it as a last resort; others call it a breach of respect; Kitwood-influenced writers worry about personhood; people with dementia and carers (Casey) accept white lies conditionally. No consensus exists on *when*. [V]
3. **Validation therapy**: widely recommended, **insufficient trial evidence** (Cochrane). Absence of evidence is not evidence of harm. [V]
4. **Reality orientation**: the one Cochrane review that found benefit is withdrawn; newer evidence is on cognitive stimulation therapy instead. Whether confronting facts helps or harms in everyday conversation is untested. [V]
5. **Time-shifting** mechanisms (hippocampal atrophy vs network disruption vs normal reminiscence-bump effects) are still theories; the Ribot gradient is shallow and inconsistent in AD. [V]
6. **Sundowning**: no agreed definition or diagnostic criteria; prevalence from ~2% to ~66%+; causes unproven; bright-light therapy not shown to work; melatonin conflicting. [V]
7. **Telling vs not telling about a death**: no empirical test of repeated disclosure vs. avoidance. Whether repeated grief is truly "new each time" or leaves lingering emotional residue is not settled (emotional memories can persist after the facts are forgotten, [B] with some research support I did not verify here).
8. **Wandering figures**: "60%" and "50% within 24 hours" are repeated everywhere, but the primary evidence is unclear; studies range 11-50%. [V]
9. **Technology**: little RCT evidence for GPS, door alerts, fall sensors, or voice assistants in dementia; ethics of tracking and consent are unsettled. [V]
10. **Respite and carer interventions**: Cochrane review found no demonstrated benefit but trials were few and weak. Other interventions untested here. [V]
11. **Whose "reality" is it?** Different cultures treat talking with the dead, spirits, or ancestors differently. Some "deceased relative" talk may be grief or comfort, not delusion. [B] Not researched here.
12. **Stage and type**: most advice is generic. Dementia with Lewy bodies, frontotemporal dementia and vascular dementia can differ in hallucinations, insight and behaviour. [B] Not researched here.
